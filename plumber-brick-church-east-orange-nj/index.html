Plumber in Brick Church, East Orange, NJ

Brick Church is the part of East Orange built around its busier rail stop. The Brick Church station on the NJ Transit Morris & Essex lines sits at Brick Church Plaza, between Halsted Street and South Harrison Street. Main Street is the commercial corridor that serves the station, and Interstate 280 runs beside it.

This page is about plumbing in the kind of buildings that cluster around a transit stop: apartment buildings, small shops and restaurants, and newer apartment complexes. It describes how building-wide problems usually behave in general and how owners, managers and tenants can handle them. It makes no claim about the condition of any particular building. If you have a problem at a specific address, call (973) 488-5410 and talk it through.

What The Station Area Is Made Of

The city's Master Plan treats the area as a Transit Village and names an Evergreen/Halsted sub-district near the station. The station is on the National Register of Historic Places. It is a Tudor Revival structure built in 1921 and 1922 and listed in 1984, and it takes its name from the former Brick Presbyterian Church one block away.

Citywide, about three quarters of homes are in multi-unit buildings and roughly two thirds of occupied homes are rented. Press coverage documents a large new apartment redevelopment called The Crossings at Brick Church Station. That project is new construction handled by its own builders, and we say nothing about its plumbing. Official neighborhood boundaries for Brick Church are not published, so we do not draw a line around it.

What Building-Wide Plumbing Problems Look Like

In any multi-unit building, many fixtures share a few pipes. A shared riser carries water up through the stacked kitchens and baths, and a shared drain stack carries waste down. When one of those lines has a problem, the symptom often appears in several apartments at once, or in an apartment that is not the source.

The classic sign is a leak that shows up in the unit below. A stain on a ceiling can come from the unit directly above, from a riser in a wall, or from a drain line running between floors. Finding the true source takes more than patching the ceiling, which is where leak detection helps by tracing the water back before anyone opens a wall.

Boilers And Building Water Heaters

Many apartment buildings heat and make hot water centrally. That means one failed boiler or one failed building water heater can take service from every unit it feeds. Symptoms include no heat on several floors or hot water that runs out early.

For heat, citywide rules matter. In rentals the heat season runs from October 1 to May 15, with at least 68 F by day and 65 F at night under N.J.A.C. 5:10-14.4. A central system problem during that window is urgent for owners, so boiler repair and water heater repair questions are worth a call early rather than late.

Small Commercial Spaces: Grease And Drain Lines

Small shops and restaurants along a corridor like Main Street often share a building with apartments above. In general, a food business sends fats, oils and starches down its drains, and those settle and harden in the line over time. Slow sinks, gurgling floor drains and odors are the usual warnings.

When a commercial drain backs up, it can reach the apartments above or beside it, because they share the same building drain. Regular cleaning and, where a line is heavily coated, hydro-jetting are the general tools for that, and our commercial plumbing page explains the wider picture for business owners.

How Managers And Tenants Should Report And Document

A clear report speeds up every repair. Tenants should tell the manager or owner in writing, and keep a copy, as soon as they see water, a stain, a smell or a loss of heat or hot water. Managers should pass the report on quickly and note who was told and when.

  • Photograph the stain, drip or damage with a date, and photograph it again if it grows.
  • Write down which unit, which room and which fixture, plus when you first noticed it.
  • Note neighbors who also have symptoms, since several units often point to a shared line.
  • Keep every message, work order number and date of contact in one place.
  • If water is active, close the shutoff for your fixture or unit if you can reach it safely, and tell the manager at once.

Managers can also keep a simple record of shutoff locations for each riser and each unit. When a leak starts, that record saves minutes.

Coordinating Work In Occupied Buildings

Work in an occupied building affects people who did not cause the problem. A riser repair may need the water off to a whole line of apartments for a period. A drain job may need access to several units, or to a commercial space that is closed to the public.

Good coordination means the manager sets the access times and tells tenants ahead, and the plumber confirms the scope on site before work starts. Ordinary maintenance work needs no permit under N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.7, but plumbing work in New Jersey must be done by a master plumber, so for the permit on a specific job, confirm with the Building Division at 973-266-5320.

Where The Water And Sewer Lines Divide

Citywide, the East Orange Water Commission runs the water and collects the sanitary sewer. The line from the street to the curb box is the Commission's responsibility, and beyond the curb box is the owner's. The sewer lateral from the building to the Commission main is also the owner's, and the Commission can be reached at 973-266-8869.

For a building owner, that split decides who to call about a failure at the street versus inside the property line. We cannot tell from here where any one building's responsibility ends, so check on site. Call (973) 488-5410 to ask about plumbing service at your address.
